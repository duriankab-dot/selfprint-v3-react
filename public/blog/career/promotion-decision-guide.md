# Should You Take That Promotion? Twin Insights

You got the call. The promotion offer is real. More title, more responsibility, more money.

Your instinct: **Say yes immediately.**

But your Twin knows something most people miss: **Not all promotions are good decisions.**

Some promotions are traps disguised as opportunities. Some pull you away from what you're actually good at. Some create problems you didn't anticipate.

This decision deserves 48 hours of thinking before you answer.

## The Promotion Trap: Three Red Flags

### Red Flag 1: You're Being Promoted Out of Your Strength

You're an incredible individual contributor. You ship features. Customers love what you build.

The promotion: "We want you to manage people now."

This is the most common trap. Companies see talent and assume it translates to leadership. It doesn't always.

**The question:** Is this promotion promoting you *into* something you want, or *away from* something you're great at?

Some people are happier as senior individual contributors than as mediocre managers. Your Twin's job is to help you see that clearly.

### Red Flag 2: The Promotion Solves Company Problems, Not Your Career

The team is a mess. Turnover is high. Someone needs to fix it.

"You should take the promotion," they say. "We need strong leadership."

Translation: "We need you to fix our broken team."

That's different from: "This promotion will help you grow as a leader."

One is solving their problem. The other is advancing your career. They're not the same.

**The question:** Would this promotion matter to your career even if the team weren't broken?

### Red Flag 3: The Compensation Doesn't Match the Scope

New title, new responsibility, leadership of 5 people... and only a 5% raise.

In most cases, managing people requires 15-25% more compensation. If they're offering less, they either:
- Don't actually value leadership
- Don't have the budget
- Are hoping you'll accept less because of the prestige

**The question:** Does the pay align with market rate for this role?

## The Twin Framework: Evaluating a Promotion

When a promotion lands on your desk, run it through this evaluation:

```
PROMOTION: Senior Developer → Engineering Manager (5-person team)

CAREER GROWTH:
✓ Would I want this role at any company?
  Yes—engineering management is a real skill I want to develop

✓ Does this move me toward my 3-year vision?
  If 3-year vision is "Lead a team," then yes
  If 3-year vision is "Ship great software," then maybe no

✓ Will I learn things I value?
  Yes—team dynamics, communication, hiring

COMPENSATION & BENEFITS:
✓ Is the salary increase realistic? (15%+ for management)
  Offered 8%. That's below market. Worth negotiating

✓ Are other benefits improving?
  Budget for your team, conference budget, PTO?

✓ How does equity change (if startup)?
  Options vesting? Acceleration?

SUSTAINABILITY:
✓ Is the team's churn rate normal? (Red flag: team is broken)
  15% yearly churn. That's typical

✓ Do I respect the people I'd be managing?
  Yes, mostly. One person might be difficult

✓ Is my manager supportive?
  Generally yes, but hands-off

✓ Could I do this for 2 years without burning out?
  Uncertain. Management requires energy I'm not sure I have

REVERSIBILITY:
If this doesn't work out, can I go back to IC work?
  Some risk. After 2 years as manager, IC opportunities 
  might be harder to find. But if I'm strategic about it, 
  probably reversible

OVERALL ASSESSMENT:
This is a real opportunity with some red flags.
Worth pursuing—but negotiate salary + get expectations clear.
```

## What to Negotiate Before Accepting

Don't just accept the promotion as offered. Ask for:

1. **Clarity on expectations**
   "What does success look like in year 1?"
   (If they say "fix the team's problems," red flag.)

2. **Investment in your management growth**
   "Will you provide management training or coaching?"
   (Good companies do; bad companies assume you'll figure it out.)

3. **Competitive compensation**
   "This is 8% increase, which is below market for this role. Can we discuss adjustment?"

4. **Your team's capacity**
   "How much support will I get if I need to make changes to the team?"
   (If they say "figure it out," you're on your own.)

5. **A clear path back**
   "If after 2 years I want to return to individual contribution, is that possible?"
   (Forward-thinking companies say yes. Others shut the door.)

## When to Actually Say No to a Promotion

**Say no if:**
- You don't want the role; you just want the title/money
- The team is broken and they expect you to fix it on your own
- The compensation is insulting compared to market rate
- Your manager is disengaged and won't support you
- You value your current work more than the promotion
- The promotion pulls you away from your actual career path

**Saying no is a career move too.** It signals you think strategically, not reactively.

Example: "I appreciate the offer. I'm not ready to move into management now. I'd like to stay in my current role and continue growing as a principal engineer instead. Can we revisit this in 2 years?"

Good companies respect that clarity.

## Key Takeaways

- Not all promotions are good decisions for *you*
- Watch for red flags: promoting out of strength, solving company problems, inadequate compensation
- Evaluate using: career growth, compensation, sustainability, reversibility
- Negotiate before accepting (expectations, support, compensation, exit strategy)
- Saying no to a bad promotion is smarter than saying yes
