# Negotiating Your Salary: A Twin's Approach

Most people approach salary negotiation with one emotion: **fear.**

Fear they'll ask for too much and lose the offer.
Fear they'll ask for too little and leave money on the table for years.
Fear the conversation will be awkward.

Your Twin knows that fear is the enemy of negotiation. Clear thinking is what works.

Here's the truth nobody tells you: **The company already has a number in mind.** Your job isn't to convince them to pay more. Your job is to find out what that number actually is—and then decide if it's worth it.

## Why Salary Negotiation Matters

That extra $10,000 a year doesn't sound like much in a single year conversation. But negotiated salary is the difference between:

- $60,000 × 40 years = $2.4 million (uncompounded)
- $70,000 × 40 years = $2.8 million

Over your career, that $10,000 negotiation compounds into hundreds of thousands of dollars—plus benefits, PTO, and equity implications.

**One conversation. Career-changing consequences.**

Most people leave negotiation money on the table because they were scared to ask.

Your Twin's job is to separate the fear from the facts.

## The Twin Framework: Three Stages of Salary Negotiation

### Stage 1: Research (Before They Ask Your Salary)

This is where most people fail. They don't know their market value, so they throw out a number and hope.

**What to research:**

1. **Market Rate for Your Role**
   - Glassdoor salary ranges
   - Levels.fyi (especially accurate for tech)
   - Payscale reports for your location
   - Talk to 2-3 people in similar roles (networks matter)

   Aim for: A range, not a single number. "This role typically pays $80-100k in my market."

2. **This Company's Typical Offers**
   - Are they known for paying top-of-market or bottom?
   - Did friends get lowball offers here?
   - How much do entry-level vs. senior versions of the same role differ?

3. **Your Actual Leverage**
   - Do you have competing offers? (Most powerful)
   - Are you solving a critical pain point they have?
   - Is this a hot field where talent is scarce?
   - How desperate are they to fill the role?

Your leverage determines how aggressive to be. No leverage = reasonable ask. Strong leverage = push harder.

### Stage 2: The Initial Offer (Don't Accept Immediately)

They make an offer. Your instinct is to say yes immediately or accept within 24 hours.

**Don't.**

Here's the move your Twin recommends:

"Thank you so much for the offer. I'm excited about the role. I need a few days to think through all aspects—compensation, benefits, equity, start date, everything. Can I get back to you by [specific date]?"

This accomplishes three things:

1. **Buys time** to think clearly, not react emotionally
2. **Signals you're thoughtful**, not desperate
3. **Creates space** to do your actual research on whether the number makes sense

Use these days to:
- Double-check market rates for this specific role in this market
- Calculate your actual cost of living increase if relocating
- Understand the equity component (if startup)
- Decide: Is this number reasonable? If not, what is?

### Stage 3: The Negotiation Conversation

Now you have two scripts. Pick the one that fits your situation:

**Script A (When the Offer is Below Market)**

"Thank you for the offer of $75,000. I'm genuinely excited about the role and the team. Based on my research, similar roles in [market] with [your experience] typically range from $90-110k. I was hoping we could discuss something closer to $95k, given [specific value you bring]."

Note what you did:
- Appreciated the offer
- Cited data (not emotion)
- Gave a reason for your ask
- Named a specific number (not a range)

**Script B (When the Offer Surprises You—Usually Positively)**

"Thank you for this generous offer. I'm thrilled about the opportunity. Before I say yes, can we discuss [one specific thing]: the equity structure, the professional development budget, or the remote work policy?"

Sometimes salary is fixed, but benefits and perks aren't. This opens space to negotiate total compensation without appearing greedy about base pay.

**Script C (When You Have a Competing Offer)**

"I'm very interested in this role. I want to be direct: I have another offer at $95k. I'd prefer to work here because [specific reason—team, mission, growth]. Can we match or exceed that number?"

This is the nuclear option. Use it when true. Don't bluff.

## What Happens Next?

The company responds in one of four ways:

1. **"Yes, we can do $95k."**
   Congratulations. You just made an extra $20k. Your Twin was right.

2. **"We can meet you at $85k, but not higher."**
   Now you decide: Is $85k good enough? Remember your research. If you said market is $90-110k, then $85k is low. You can counter: "I appreciate the adjustment. I was hoping to get closer to $90k. What would that take?"

3. **"We can't move on salary, but we can add [vacation days, flexible hours, signing bonus, extra equity]."**
   Now you evaluate: Is what they're offering worth the salary compromise?

4. **"This is our final offer. Take it or leave it."**
   This signals they're not flexible. Time to decide: Is the job worth this number, or should you walk?

Most companies have wiggle room. Not all of them will use it. Your job is to ask clearly, then decide if their answer is acceptable.

## The One Negotiation Mistake to Avoid

You over-negotiate and they rescind the offer.

Here's the reality: **This almost never happens.** Companies invest resources in making offers. They won't withdraw because you asked for a raise. They might say no, but they won't usually punish you for asking.

That said, read the room. If they seem genuinely final, asking a third time is annoying, not strategic.

Your Twin's principle: Negotiate once strongly. If they say no, accept or walk. Don't be that person who keeps coming back for more.

## Key Takeaways

- Research market rate before any conversation (use Levels.fyi, Glassdoor, networks)
- Never accept an offer in the moment—buy yourself 3-5 days to think
- Negotiate once, with data and a specific number
- If salary is fixed, negotiate benefits, equity, or flexibility
- Accept their answer or walk; don't re-litigate

## How Your Twin Guides You

Salary negotiation is just another decision. You have incomplete information. You need to gather data, evaluate options, and choose deliberately.

Your Twin separates the fear ("What if they're mad?") from the reality ("They're negotiating compensation with everyone").

The best negotiators aren't aggressive. They're calm, informed, and clear about what they want.
