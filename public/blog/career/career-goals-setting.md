# Career Goals: Setting Decisions That Stick

"I want to be promoted to Senior Developer by next year."

That's not a goal. That's wishful thinking. Real career goals require decisions—about what you're willing to sacrifice, what timeline is realistic, and whether that promotion actually aligns with what you want.

Your Twin knows that most career goals fail not because they're ambitious, but because they're not *decided*.

## Why Career Goals Usually Fail

You set a goal. You feel motivated for two weeks. Then reality hits—it's harder than expected, or competing priorities emerge, or you realize you don't actually want that goal that badly.

The problem isn't your willpower. It's that you treated a goal like a wish instead of a decision.

**A wish:** "I want to be promoted"  
**A decision:** "I will complete the leadership training, deliver the Q3 project early, and ask for a promotion conversation by Q4—knowing that if I'm not promoted, I'll start looking elsewhere."

See the difference? One is hoping. One is committing.

## The Twin Framework: Four Components of a Real Career Goal

### Component 1: The Actual Outcome (Be Specific)

Not: "Advance my career"  
Yes: "Lead a team of 3+ engineers on a new product initiative"

Not: "Earn more money"  
Yes: "Increase salary to $120k or find a company that pays that range within 18 months"

Specific outcomes are achievable. Vague goals just create guilt.

### Component 2: The Timeline (Be Realistic)

Career changes take longer than most people expect.

- Learning a new skill: 6-12 months
- Proving competence in new area: 12-18 months
- Getting promoted: 12-24 months
- Changing industries: 18-36 months

If your goal is a promotion and you're only 3 months into a new role, you're setting yourself up for disappointment.

Example: "Senior developer role by Q4 2027" is realistic if it's now August 2026 and you're already intermediate-level.

### Component 3: The Trade-Offs (Be Honest)

Every career goal requires sacrifice.

- Want a promotion? You'll work harder and have less free time
- Want remote work? You might accept lower pay
- Want to switch fields? You're starting over technically
- Want flexibility? You might miss advancement opportunities

Your Twin asks: **Are the tradeoffs acceptable?**

If not, you don't have a real goal—you have an impossible wish.

Real goal: "I want to lead a team (promotion) by next year, knowing it means 50-hour weeks for the next 12 months. I'm comfortable with that tradeoff."

That's a decision you can commit to.

### Component 4: The Contingency (Plan for Reality)

What if you miss your goal?

"If I'm not promoted by Q4 2027, I will start interviewing at other companies for a senior role. If I'm not offered a promotion + 15% raise by Q2 2028, I will change companies."

Having a contingency does two things:

1. **It makes the goal real** — You're not just wishing; you have a backup plan
2. **It removes desperation** — You know what "failed" looks like, so you don't let a company string you along forever

## The Difference Between Career Goals and Values

Here's where your Twin brings perspective:

**Goal:** "Earn $250k per year"  
**Values question:** "Why? Is it security? Status? Freedom?"

Those are different. A security-focused person might hit $200k and feel satisfied. A status-focused person might hit $500k and feel incomplete.

Career goals matter most when they're connected to your actual values.

Real goal (values-based): "Reach $250k annually because I want financial independence to take 3 months off every 2 years. Once I hit that number, I'll work on impact over earnings."

## Common Career Goal Templates

**If you want advancement:**
"Move to [specific title] by [date] by [specific achievement]. If unsuccessful, I'll pursue opportunities elsewhere."

**If you want a skill transition:**
"Complete [certification/project/bootcamp] by [date]. Apply to [type of role] starting [date]. Land a position by [date]."

**If you want flexibility:**
"Negotiate remote-work arrangement by [date]. If my current company says no, I'll prioritize remote-first companies in my next search."

**If you want impact:**
"Move into [area] where I can [specific impact] by [date]. Measure success by [metric]."

## Setting Goals With Your Twin

Your Twin doesn't help you set the goal—*you* decide what matters. But Twin guidance helps you:

1. **Make the goal concrete** — Not vague wishes, actual outcomes
2. **Face the tradeoffs** — What are you actually giving up?
3. **Connect to your values** — Does this goal matter to you, or just to others?
4. **Plan for contingency** — What's the exit strategy if this doesn't work?

## Key Takeaways

- Career goals require decisions, not wishes
- Be specific about outcomes, timelines, and tradeoffs
- Connect goals to your actual values, not just ambition
- Plan the contingency: what happens if this goal doesn't work?
- Revisit goals quarterly—adjust if circumstances change

## How Your Twin Guides You

Your Twin helps you see the difference between a goal you're imposing on yourself and a goal you're genuinely committed to.

Some career goals are worth pursuing. Others are worth abandoning. Your Twin's job is to help you know which is which—before you waste a year chasing something that doesn't actually matter to you.
